The Problem
Why 2D navigation falls short

Traditional navigation apps like Google Maps and Waze rely on 2D map visualization, voice guidance and live traffic updates. But they share a well-documented usability issue: drivers have to repeatedly switch attention between the road ahead and a flat digital map, increasing cognitive load and reducing situational awareness.

The project set out to test whether augmented reality navigation — projecting arrows and route lines directly onto the driver's real-world view through the camera — could reduce that distraction compared to conventional 2D map navigation, while remaining reliable enough for real driving conditions.

What It Does
Core features
Real-time AR overlays

Arrows, route lines and distance markers rendered on the live camera feed via ARCore, aligned to the driver's actual field of view.

Google Maps SDK routing

Point-to-point route generation, mapping and GPS tracking, so navigation logic stays accurate even as AR handles the visual layer.

Tiered voice guidance

Far, mid, and imminent-tier voice announcements timed to the upcoming turn, with mute state synced across screens and persisted across restarts.

Graceful degradation

Handles GPS signal loss and internet disconnection without crashing, and shows a visual safety overlay when signal is weak.

Live rerouting

Detects off-route deviation mid-drive and recalculates the path automatically, tested and confirmed on a real 24km route.

Portrait-locked AR tracking

Deliberately scoped to portrait mode only, since ARCore tracking is measurably more stable in that orientation.

Proving It Works
Testing & results

Four rounds of testing were run to check the app against its functional and usability goals: functional, performance, usability, and a real-world field test.

Functional
67 / 69 test cases passed outright, 2 partial, 0 failed — covering startup, search, AR navigation, direction & distance accuracy, rerouting and error handling.
Performance
Met all non-functional requirements with reasonable margin. Battery drain and device heat were noted, but didn't affect core functionality.
Usability
Survey respondents rated the app consistently above the midpoint with no major weaknesses flagged. First-time AR users needed a short adjustment period.
Field test
A real 24km, 30-minute drive — 90% accuracy, no overlay drift, successful rerouting, and stable AR tracking through a tunnel.

Being Honest About Trade-offs
Known limitations
HardwareRequires an ARCore-supported Android device with a working camera, gyroscope and GPS.
EnvironmentLighting, GPS drift and camera calibration can affect overlay alignment; heavy rain or fog can degrade tracking.
ConnectivityRoute data depends on real-time internet access to the Google Maps API.
Battery / heatContinuous camera + GPS use during AR sessions drains battery faster and can heat the device.
ScopePoint-to-point navigation only — no gesture control or AI-based route optimization in this version.
Takeaway
What the results actually showed

The core navigation experience — route generation, AR overlay rendering, direction and distance accuracy — held up reliably under real outdoor driving conditions. On distraction reduction specifically, results showed AR navigation performing roughly on par with 2D maps, with a slight lean in AR's favor rather than a dramatic improvement — a modest but genuine result, likely tempered by most test participants trying an AR navigation app for the first time.

Taken together, the functional, performance, usability and field results supported the project's two goals: a technically reliable AR navigation system, and a measurable usability improvement over conventional 2D navigation.
